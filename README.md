# Garden of Delight

A compact English cottage garden (about 54 × 86 m, in a field inside a space bubble, waiting for you to wall it) lit for a night exhibition — fairy-lit trees, fields of glowing stalks, frosted globes, a tunnel of light arches — with ten black holes of different sizes hanging in it: pinholes nesting in tree canopies that drain a single bush, larger ones over the walks, and a giant hanging about 16 m above the great fountain that you can walk beneath.

Each void sleeps as a small dark bead. Walk close enough and it wakes (bigger voids notice you from farther away — ~10 m for a pinhole, ~28 m for the giant): it grows, lenses the scene behind it, twists and pulls nearby lights toward its horizon, drains the light from the ground, trees and globes around it, and draws streams of stolen light spiralling in. It also tugs on you. Walk away and the light seeps back over the next half-minute.

**Play:** open `index.html` (or the GitHub Pages site). The page opens outside the bubble, watching it go by, with only the title over it while it loads. Enter (the first time, the story so far plays first: see below), and the view holds a moment longer, then flies down through the skin and the trees into your own eyes.

- **Space view (Z, or the button):** the camera leaves your eyes, passes out through the bubble's skin and takes up an orbit outside it. From there the bubble is a soap-film sphere with the land hanging in it (garden and wood on top, the torn rock beneath), and dust streaming past from the nebula it is heading for. Drag circles it, the wheel or a pinch comes nearer; left alone the view drifts round. Z again brings you back.
- Desktop: WASD / arrows walk, mouse looks (click to capture, Esc to release), Shift runs (the ordinary pace is a walk), **E** opens a new void of random size ahead of you, **M** toggles sound. The **pull** slider sets how hard the voids tug on you (0–150%). **F** shows the frame rate and render resolution.
- Time of day: the pill at bottom right toggles **night** (default) ↔ **live** (the real sky right now: your clock and today's date; also **T**), and its slider scrubs to any hour on the garden's own calendar date. The sun is placed by a solar-position calculation (≈42°N, longitude from your time zone), with day sky, clouds, twilight and stars. In daylight the voids carve pockets of night out of the day.
- Touch (iPad, iPhone): drag the left half to walk (a quick flick up on it jumps), the right half to look, double-tap to open a void. A rail of mode buttons down the right edge goes straight to **walk**, **plant**, **build**, **space**, **me** (see yourself) and **gesture**; tap the lit one to come back to walking. Beside it is a pad of the buttons that mode needs (walking: jump, void, vaporize, lift; building: lift/set, turn, fine, tip, roll, length in, on edge, press, pass through, hearting, more stone, glue, undo, finish, put away; planting: the plant list, dig up, done). Each button sends the same key or click as the keyboard and the controller (`src/touchui.js`).
- **Jump: Space** (✕ / A on a controller). Gravity in the bubble is 4.2 m/s², a little under half the Earth's, so a hop lifts you about 1.4 m and takes 1.6 s to come down. Voids open with E.
- **Climbing.** Walk into any tree's trunk and keep going: after a moment you take hold (in mid-jump you catch hold at once). W or the stick up climbs, S comes down (at the foot you are walking again), A and D go round the trunk, Shift is faster. Up as far as the crown, till your head is out of it; Space lets go, a little out from the bark, and you come down in the bubble's weak pull. Works on the garden's trees, the wood and any tree you plant.
- **Look down** and you are there: a future astronaut in a hard-shell suit, cel-shaded with ink outlines. It walks and runs with you, reaches out when you click a void, and casts your shadow, helmet and pack included.
- **The hands are jointed** (`src/hands.js`): each glove has four fingers of three shells and a thumb of three, posed every frame from a few numbers per hand (each digit's curl, the fingers' spread, the thumb across the palm, the wrist's flex, bend and roll). Gestures set the targets and slightly underdamped springs carry the hand there, so it settles rather than snaps; on top of that the hand swings and lags with its own inertia when the arm swings or stops. Walking: a loose relaxed curl, tightening toward a fist as you run. A void: the hand opens, spread, then closes on it as it goes. At the wall: the left hand holds the console up, open and easy; the right is open and ready, opens wide with the palm to a stone you point at, closes on a held stone in a grip that tightens and trembles with the strain, eases as you let it down, goes flat with the wrist back when you press (Space), rolls into a turn, and ripples while a stone passes through (G). With the trowel out it points. `body.hands.pose(side)` gives a hand's state as eleven numbers and `drive(side, pose)` plays one back: the hook for showing other players' hands.
- **They are alive when idle, too:** the fingers drift on two slow rhythms, open a little as the arm swings forward, and every few seconds, when you aren't working, a hand does something small of its own (rolls its fingers, stretches, flexes, rubs a thumb across the fingertips).
- **The walk is a person's, not a machine's** (`src/body.js`): you rise over the stiff leg mid-stride walking and sink onto a bent one running; the hips sway over the foot that carries you and drop on the swinging side; the spine turns against the hips a beat late; you lean into starts and stops and bank into turns; no two strides are quite the same length, width or height; the toes turn out a little. Walking, the arms hang loose and nearly straight and swing from the shoulder, further forward than back, the forearm a beat behind; running (Shift), the elbows bend, the hands pump across the body and the heels kick up. Standing, the weight moves from foot to foot now and then. **Shift+V** turns the camera round so you can watch yourself (again to come back); the angle and distance are `__umbra.selfie(on, angle, distance)`.

## The field, the bubble and the wall

The garden stands in the middle of a field about two football pitches across (212 m), and the field is inside a bubble of air travelling through space. The land is a piece of ground torn out whole: an iceberg of earth, upside down, hanging in the bubble clear of its skin. The ground rolls a little beyond the garden's level plateau and ends, 94 to 104 m out, in a ragged brink where the turf is ripped off. Walk to it and look over: below is a cliff of soil and hanging roots cut back under the lip, then bedded rock narrowing in fins to a keel about 70 m down, and past that the bubble's lower skin and whatever it is flying through. Stars and dust show below the land as well as above it. Most of the field is a wood: about 180 trees of the grove's eight species, in stands, thrown down with eight to ten metres between trunks. Left open are a band about 13 m wide round the garden (for the wall and its stone), a ride running south from the gate to the rim, and four glades for the sky. The first rank of the wood is grown in full; the trees behind stand on plainer limbs and carry every bough too (Graphics → Wood: *half* gives them every other bough, the default on the phone and laptop presets). Overhead, by night and thinly by day, is what the bubble is passing through: a golden nebula dead ahead, wisps elsewhere, and dust streaming back from the point you are heading for. The skin itself is a soap film: banded with colour low down where it has drained thick, almost nothing overhead, bright where it turns away from you and where it meets the ground.

The brick wall is gone, and building its replacement is the long task: a **dry stone wall** on the same line, 277 m of it in 92 lengths ("stints") of about three metres, pegged out with strings. The gate piers are left standing as the two ends. The stones, their physics and the reading of the wall are [Dry Stack](https://github.com/samuelling/dry-stack)'s (`src/drystack/`, with Rapier 0.14 in `vendor/rapier/`; it is fetched only when you begin your first stint, and from jsDelivr where the vendored copy is not served).

- Four great heaps of fieldstone lie at the corners of the walling band, about a thousand stones each: big stones laid first wherever they do not sit on another, smaller ones in the gaps, hearting in what is left, over a buried layer so the heaps read as solid. **Every stone in them can be lifted out** (see below). Walk up to a pegged length and press **B**. Your suit brings the stone to the field side of the line (about 330 pieces: foundations, builders, throughs, copes, hearting, pins), sets up batter frames and string lines, and treads the grass down so you can see what you have.
- You do not hold the stones. The suit lifts what your right hand points at: point at a stone and **click** to lift it, look where it should go, click again to set it down. A lifted stone is still a real body. It lags when it is heavy, it shoves and is shoved, and it settles where it is put. The lift is limited (520 N), so a 50 kg foundation moves slowly and the console's lift bar fills.
- **Wheel** or **Q / E** turn the stone, **R** tips it forward, **V** rolls it, **T** lays its length into the wall, **C** stands it on edge for a cope (Shift for finer steps). **Space** presses it down, **G** lets it pass through others while held, **H** pours a few pieces of hearting where you point, **N** brings more stone, **Backspace** takes back the last move.
- Your left forearm comes up and its wrist display throws a small hologram into the lower left of your view (lettering only, in scanlines, with a fan of light from the wrist; it idles dim and brightens when what it says changes): the stone in hand and its weight, the lift, and how the stint stands (footings, height against the 1.1 m target, hearting, throughs, running joints, copes).
- **P** puts the stint away as it lies, to take up again later; walking 40 m off does the same. **Enter** twice finishes it: what stands in the wall is fixed in place and you can no longer walk through that length; the rest of the pile goes back. Finished and half-built stints are kept in this browser (`localStorage`), with every stone where you left it.
- Settings → **Wall**: the stone (six kinds, for the next stint you begin), progress, the junk heap, taking down your free builds, and pulling the whole wall down.

### Free building, and building from the heaps

The stints are the guided way. You can also build anywhere, however you like:

- **B** away from the wall line opens a *free build* where you stand: no frames, no strings, no reading of the wall. Everything you set stays where it lies. **N** brings a pile of stone in front of you (builders, a few foundations and throughs, a heap of hearting); **P** or **Enter** twice puts the site away, kept just as it is, and **B** inside it again takes it back up. A put-away free build is solid: you walk round it.
- **Lifting from the heaps.** Point at a stone in any of the four fieldstone heaps, or a piece on the junk heap, and click. If nothing is open, that opens a site where you stand (the stint you are at, the free build you are in, or a new free build) and lifts it. With a site open, heap stones are pointed at and lifted like the pile's. The stone comes out of the heap as itself (the same shape, now a real body), and the heap remembers it is gone. A stone from a heap that is not in the wall when you finish a stint goes back to its place; taking down a free build gives its heap stones and junk back too.
- **Glue** (free builds only, as Tears of the Kingdom's Ultrahand): press a piece against others and **J** welds it to everything it touches: a fixed joint at each seam, marked with a green blob. Glued pieces move as one: lift any of them and the whole assembly comes with it, weightless in the suit's beam while held, and lands as one piece. **Shift+J** unglues the piece you hold or look at. Glue is saved with the build. A dry stone wall takes no glue.
- **Ultrahand** (free builds; Tears of the Kingdom's). What you lift hangs in the air where you look, at the distance it was when you took it: the wheel (or **-** and **=**) brings it nearer or sends it farther. **Q E** turn it an eighth of a turn, **R** tips and **V** rolls it about the way you are looking (with Shift, a twenty-fourth). A ring on the ground marks what is straight below it. Bring it near another piece and a green line runs between the nearest points of the two, the other piece lit; **J** pulls yours in until they touch and welds them there, and you go on holding the whole assembly. Shake what you hold from side to side, fast (five swings in under a second), and it comes apart. A click lets go, and it drops from where it hangs. The junk from the Shred Hole is the thing to build with: every piece is its own hull and mass. On an iPad the pad has nearer, farther, turn, tip, roll, stick and unglue; on a controller the d-pad is farther, nearer, tip and roll and R3 sticks.
- The physics stands on the real ground under a site, sampled every 30 cm over 60 m: the field's rise and fall, the brook's channel, and the heaps' mounds, so you can set stones back on a heap.

### The wormhole and the junk heap

The junk comes from the [Shred Hole](https://linglab.org/tunnel/?to=garden). Walk to the wormhole's clearing and a **🌀 Shred Hole** button comes up at the top of the screen (also Settings → Wall → *Open the Shred Hole*): it opens the Shred Hole already set to send here.


In a clearing in the wood about 25 m east of the wall's east side, level with the grand fountain, a wormhole hangs twenty-two metres up, and climbs as the heap grows so that it always stays twelve metres clear of the top of the pile. A heap that grows past 700 pieces, or higher than seven metres, is too big: the wormhole takes some of it back, vaporizing pieces at random off the top of the pile, a few a second, until it is comfortably under that again (what is built into a wall or a free build is never taken). It is drawn as a traversable (Ellis) wormhole is expected to look, the way Kip Thorne's group rendered one for *Interstellar*: not a glowing thing but a crystal ball made of bent light. It is done in the same post pass that lenses the black holes, ray by ray:

- A ray that passes the throat (radius 1.8 m) farther out than its radius stays on this side and is bent round it, by about (π/4)(a/b)² far out and without limit as it grazes the throat. So the trees, sky and grass around it are wrapped into rings of stretched and multiplied images, and things directly behind it show round its rim.
- A ray inside the throat goes through, and comes out at the far mouth, which opens in the Shred Hole's hall: the lamps round its walls, the hazard ring and the violet vortex portal below, the tower's yellow hoops and lit shaft overhead with the fan turning in it. The whole of that far sky is fitted into the disc, more and more compressed toward its edge.
- It gives off no light of its own. While a payload is coming through, the far portal flares and the pieces are seen streaming up the throat toward you before they fly out.

When someone pulls **FLUSH!** in [Shred Hole](https://linglab.org/tunnel/) with the **🌳 GARDEN** destination chosen, the payload comes out of it a few pieces at a time, arcs down and lands on a junk heap below, the hole flaring while it comes.

- Every piece is the shredder's own: its convex hull (16 points), its mass, its outer and inner colours, and the name of what it was ("VAT OF REGRETS · 0.6 kg" on the console). From mass over hull volume the suit judges its density: foam is light, slides and bounces a little; steel sits heavy. By size it is a through (over 60 cm), a builder, hearting or a pin, so it counts in a stint's reading like stone.
- The garden reads the Shred Hole's mailbox every 25 s while the page is open, and only the drops marked for the garden; the first visit looks back two weeks. The heap is kept in this browser, up to 1,400 pieces (the oldest that are not built into anything go first). Each browser gets the same drops, so everyone's shreds land in everyone's garden.
- **Whole things** (`src/whole.js`): anything that reaches the portal unbroken (Shred Hole's **📦 WHOLE** switch, or something too tough for its fan) brings its recipe, and comes out as itself: the duck, the couch, the hatchback, built from the same parts the Shred Hole builds it from (spheres, boxes, lathes, tubes, the teapot), in its own colours and printed patterns, with each material's shine (chrome reflects, rubber doesn't, a glow stick glows; glass is pale but not see-through). It tumbles out slowly and lands on its feet round the edge of the heap, bigger things further out. It can be lifted and built with like any piece, by its convex hull and its real mass (a 1,350 kg car is beyond the suit's lift). A hundred rubber ducks keep one copy of the duck's recipe.
- **Vaporizing.** Look at a piece of junk (with nothing open and the trowel away) and **X** vaporizes it, for good, in a blue-white flash. Settings → Wall → *Vaporize the whole junk heap* clears it all; what you have built with stays.
- **Walk me to the wormhole** in Settings → Wall puts you at the heap; Shred Hole's 🌀 does the same when it is set to the garden (`?at=wormhole`).

Not modelled for junk: material (the drop carries none, so friction and bounce are guessed from density), and shape detail beyond the 16-point hull.

Not in this version: the hammer and the fit light from Dry Stack, and any collision between you and loose stones (you walk through the pile).

## Guns

The Shred Hole has an **Arsenal** at the end of its shelf: Betamax's guns (SIG M7, IWI Tavor X95, SIG M250, IWI Negev NG7, HK416 A5, HK MP7A2, Benelli M1014, HK M110A1 CSASS, SIG M18, Glock 17). Gun steel doesn't shred, so they go through the portal whole, and in the garden each comes out of the wormhole as the real gun (Betamax's own models, `src/betamax/`), falling to the grass by the junk heap.

For now (a demo, before anything has come through) there's also a **crate of the whole arsenal** on the path just inside the gate, ahead and to the right where you start. **Q** at it takes a gun; Q again swaps it for the next one. It never runs out, and it's the same crate in every browser, so it isn't saved or sent (`?nocrate` leaves it out).

- **Q** picks up the gun you look at (putting down the one you hold), or draws and puts away your own; **Shift+Q** puts it down. **Click** fires (held, for the automatics), the **right button** aims down the sight, **R** reloads. On an iPad the pad has *gun: pick up / draw*, then fire (hold), aim (hold), reload and put away; on a controller L3 picks up, R2 fires, L2 aims, □ reloads.
- Each gun keeps Betamax's numbers: rate of fire, magazine, spread (more moving, much less aimed), recoil, the shotgun's nine pellets, damage. Its report is synthesized the way Betamax does it (fitted to recordings of real rifles).
- **Holding it.** Your suit's own arms hold the gun (`src/body.js`, solved to it every frame): the right glove round the pistol grip, index finger along the guard, the left cupping the fore-end, the torso bladed into it and the spine held upright. It's the same body in first person, in the mirror view (Shift+V) and on everyone else in a room. The suit's arms are shorter than Betamax's view-model arms, so the gun is carried a little closer, and on the longest guns the left hand slides back along the fore-end rather than float.
- **Reloading** is Betamax's own choreography played on those arms: the rifle comes in to the workspace canted, the magazine drops out (a spent one falls and lies on the ground), the left hand goes to the pouch, seats a fresh one, tugs it, and slaps the bolt catch or racks the handle. A tactical reload (rounds left) keeps one in the chamber. The Benelli is loaded a shell at a time. Every step has its handling noise, and in a room the others see you do it.
- Firing is hitscan from your eye. A round stops at the first thing on its line: another player, a stone or piece of junk in an open free build (it takes the round's real momentum, about 4 to 12 N·s, and moves), a tree trunk, a lamp, the crate, the water, a hedge or the ground. Brass (a red hull from the Benelli) flies out of the port and tinkles where it lands.
- **What rounds do** (`src/havoc.js`): every round leaves a mark, a crater in the ground, a splintered hole in wood, a pale chip in stone, a bright dent in metal (marks on a stone ride on it when it's knocked over), and throws out real debris: clods, splinters, bark, stone chips, leaves, petals, glass. The debris falls, bounces, rolls to a stop and lies there a while; leaves flutter down; anything landing in water sinks. Rounds into the pools and the canal throw up a crown of spray and a ring.
  - **Plants** a round goes through are shot to pieces: two rifle rounds and a flower is gone, dug up as a trowel would, kept, and gone for everyone in a room. *Settings → Garden → Put back the garden's own plants* brings the garden's own back; your own plantings are gone for good.
  - **A tree you planted** can be cut down. Each round takes out wood; the trunk takes as many as its cross-section (a young maple, 20 cm through, about 40 rifle rounds; a giant, not with any magazine). Cut through, it falls away from you like a pole, slowly and then fast, lands with a crash and a burst of leaves, lies there, and sinks away. The garden's own trees only splinter.
  - **Lamps** shot out shatter: the glass goes, the bulb and its pool of light on the ground go dark, for everyone in the room, until the page is loaded again.
  - The marks and the litter are this visit's only; what's destroyed (plants, trees) is kept.
- **In a room you can shoot each other.** The shooter decides the hit (what you saw is what you hit) and tells the room. A body shot takes the gun's damage off 100, a head shot its head damage (an M7 round: 46, or 200 to the head). You heal after four seconds without being hit. *Settings → Garden → Others can shoot me* turns it off for you (their rounds pass through; your gun still works). Everyone sees what a hit does:
  - the body **flinches** away from the round (the gun in its hands goes with it), and your own view jolts;
  - the **suit is holed** where the round went in, a scorched ring with the lining's glow, and **leaks air** in a hissing jet for a few seconds;
  - a **head shot cracks the visor**: a star on the helmet for the others, and cracks across your own view until you're up again;
  - at nothing left you **go down**: you topple the way the round pushed you and lie there, and your view steps out to circle above where you lie. **You get up when you choose**: *get up here* (**E**) or *back to the gate* (**G**), or tap the buttons; the holes and cracks go when you do.
- The guns lying about are part of the garden: saved with it, handed over in a room, kept online.

## The story so far (the intro film)

The first time you enter, the story plays before the garden does (`src/film.js`, from [STORY.md](STORY.md)): about a minute and a half, eight shots, **told by Brodie**, with his words under them.

1. **Earth**, in the year after the one you are watching in (2027, played in 2026): its night side from orbit: the real coasts and the real city lights (NASA's Blue Marble and Black Marble), the sun a diamond ring at the limb. Then The Event crosses the world from one place: ahead of its front the cities surge, behind it they are out, and the air turns the colour of ash.
2. **The fleet**: eighty bubbles lifting off what's left and out past the camera, each a soap film round a piece of ground with a lit garden on it.
3. **Our ship**: this is the game itself, its own bubble against the nebula it is flying toward, with the dust going by. The camera drops under the brink to the torn ground, where the blueprint and the snapshot show as lights: *everyone alive in* the year after the one you are watching in, *…and gone in* that same year (the lights dim, and stay). It is always next year.
4. **The wormhole**: the one in the east field, from the air beside it, the garden lit below; things fall out of it.
5. **The black holes**: a glitch in the wormhole opened them inside the ship. Under the great void over the fountain as it wakes and bends the garden's lights; then, from outside, what that did: the ship rolling off its course, the stars swinging. They come and go as they wish.
6. **The Shred Hole's ship**: bare scorched ground, and the tower working in the middle of it under its floodlights.
7. **World Builder**: something turning, a long way off, under half-redacted coordinates.
8. **Home**: in at the gate, through the light tunnel, up over the garden; then the ship going on, and its name. It hands over to the game's own view of the bubble and the fly-in.

- **Skip** with the button, Esc, Enter or Space. It plays once: after that, **the story so far** under the title (or Settings → Garden) plays it again. `?film` forces it, `?nofilm` never plays it.
- **Half of it is the game.** For shots 3, 4, 5 and 8 the film only says where the camera is: the game draws them, with everything it has (the soap film, the lensing, the bloom). The voids notice the film's camera as they would notice you. A few things are laid into the game's scene: the lights in the ground, and what falls through the wormhole. If you replay it from Settings, those shots show your garden as it is at that moment.
- **The other half** has a scene of its own, drawn through its own bloom and the game's tone curve: the Earth (day and night pictures, a sea mask for the sun's glint, clouds and air made in the shader), the fleet (instanced), the Shred Hole's tower (modelled here, part by part), and World Builder (46,000 points on a spiral).
- **One grade over all of it**: a little lens colour toward the corners, grain, a vignette, cool shadows and warm lights, and the sun's flare; wide-screen bars. On an upright phone the lens widens so each shot keeps its width.
- **Brodie's telling** was spoken once, in his ElevenLabs voice, and is kept in `assets/film/vo/`: playing the film spends nothing. `tools/narration.json` holds the lines (the three with the year in them are there for each year to 2032: add the next three before the January they are needed; `?filmyear=2029` shows another year's telling); change one and push, and the workflow (`.github/workflows/narration.yml`) has the sphere Worker speak just that line again. It plays through his voice box (below), a touch slow.
- **The score** is synthesized as it plays (Web Audio): a drone, a chord for each shot, a sound for each event, in a long reverb. The garden's own sound waits outside until it ends.
- The pictures of Earth (about 0.9 MB) are fetched only if the film is going to play.

## Star navigation

The ship lost its navigation centuries ago. The first step to getting it back (`src/nav.js`, `src/navfix.js`, `src/navstars.js`) is the oldest one there is: the sky over the garden is the real one, so the stars can tell you where the ship is holding.

- **The sextant comes from the Shred Hole.** Its *Instruments* shelf has a **bubble sextant** (an aircraft navigator's: it carries its own horizon, a bubble, so it needs no sea). Brass doesn't shred: send it through and it falls out of the wormhole by the junk heap, glinting. **N** picks it up. (Ask Brodie about the stars before you have one and he tells you where to get it.)
- **N** takes it out and puts it away. With it out on a clear night, the 58 navigator's stars that are up are **named** in the sky. **Hold the right button (or L)** to put it to your eye: the view narrows to 12°, and the star nearest the cross is ringed.
- **The bubble.** It lags when you turn and swings back, and your hands never quite hold it still. **Click (or K)** when it has settled in its ring: that is one sight, the star's height above the horizon at that moment.
- **Climb first.** Near the ground the stars swim in the haze: a sight from the lawn is good to about ±45′ (tens of miles). The error falls away with height (it halves about every 5 m): from 15 m up a tree it is ±5′. Walk into a trunk, climb, and take the sextant out up there: you hold on with one arm and sight with the other.
- **R works the fix**, once you have three different stars. Each sight says: *you are somewhere on this line*, a line square to the star's bearing (the altitude-intercept method, Marcq St Hilaire's, 1875: compare the height you measured with the height the almanac says the star should have from a guessed position; each minute of arc of difference is one nautical mile toward the star or away). The chart draws every line, the **cocked hat** where they cross, and the least-squares position, with each star's height and how far its line lies from the fix.
- **Good sights are well spread.** Three stars about a third of the way round the sky from each other cross cleanly. Three in one part of the sky cross at thin angles and the fix slides along them; the log says so.
- **The grades**: a *navigator's fix* (within ten miles, lines crossing cleanly), *position fixed* (within thirty, with lines that cross within forty miles of each other, or six sights to average), a *rough fix*, or *not a fix yet*. The first *position fixed* brings the ship's first navigation system back, and Brodie has something to say.
- Brodie teaches it: "teach me… star navigation". On touch: the *sextant* button on the pad, then *raise / lower*, *sight*, *work the fix*. A controller has the same.
- In a room, the others see you holding it. Your sights and fixes are your own (kept in this browser).
- What the fix finds is the place whose sky the garden is showing (the same place the sun and weather use). It is drawn on your screen only.

## Brodie

**Brodie** is a little glass sphere that floats at your left shoulder (`src/sphere.js`). Inside him is the garden, small: its brick walls, the loop walk, the pools and canal, the garden's trees and the ones you plant (they turn up in his glass as you plant them), the lamps, and the black hole over it all with its glowing ring. He turns to look at you, blinks, glows when he talks, and says "brody" a lot. It's slang.

- **Company.** The first time, he says hello. Now and then, when you're not busy, he says something: about the ship and the story, the time of day, what you're holding, your plants. If you've been on your own a long while he suggests bringing a friend into a room. Settings → Garden → Brodie: chatter *only when asked*, *now and then* or *chatty*; or Brodie off.
- **Talk to him**: **I**, or the *brodie* button. He really converses (Claude Haiku, told where you are, what you're doing and the time) with either of two keys:
  - **your own Anthropic API key**: Settings → Garden → *Use my own Anthropic API key*. It's kept in this browser and sent only to Anthropic (`api.anthropic.com`, straight from the page). Note that every site under linglab.org shares one browser storage, so any of them could read it.
  - **the garden's key**, behind the sphere Worker (below), for everyone else.
  - With neither (or offline) he answers from his written lines, and says so once.
- **Lessons**: say "teach me…" (or ask him how to do something) and he walks you through getting about, planting, the dry stone wall, free builds, climbing trees, guns or star navigation, a step at a time. Each step is done when you've actually done it; you can skip a step or stop.
- **His voice** (Settings → Garden → Brodie → Voice): off, the browser's own (free, a bit robotic), or **Brodie's**, an ElevenLabs voice spoken through the sphere Worker (it falls back to the browser's).
- **His voice box** (`src/voicefx.js`): Brodie is a small glass machine, so his own voice goes through a **harmonizer, half mixed**: what he said, plus two copies of it, a fourth below and a fifth above, with a short metallic ring. (The copies are made the way rack harmonizers made them: a delay line whose length is swept by a sawtooth, so what comes out is played slower or faster than it went in; two such taps half a sweep apart, cross-faded so the jump is never heard.) *Robot in his voice* in Settings sets the mix: 0 is his plain voice, 50% the default, 100% all machine. The browser's voice can't be routed through it.
- He's for everyone, children included: what he says (written, or from Haiku, `src/brodie.js` holds who he is) is kind and simple, he never asks for personal information, and if you tell him you're lonely he's glad of you and nudges you toward the people in your life.

**The sphere Worker** (`worker-sphere/`, `garden-sphere`) holds the keys so the page never does: `POST /chat` is Claude Haiku as Brodie, `POST /speak` is his ElevenLabs voice, `GET /health` says which are set. It only answers the garden's own sites (`ORIGINS`), twelve asks a minute per address. It is deployed by Workers Builds from this repo (root `/worker-sphere`), so a push redeploys it. Its keys are secrets you set in the dashboard (garden-sphere → Settings → Variables and Secrets): `ANTHROPIC_API_KEY`, and for his voice `ELEVENLABS_API_KEY`. His voice is ElevenLabs' Luis (`BRODIE_VOICE`). The page already looks for it at `https://garden-sphere.samuelling.workers.dev` (or `?sphere=<url>`). A spending limit on the Anthropic console caps what it can cost.

## Keeping your garden

Your garden is kept in your browser, and online (`src/worlds.js`, and the `garden-worlds` Cloudflare Worker in `worker/`):

- **Your own garden** is saved online every 20 seconds while it changes and the page is in front, under a secret link made the first time (Settings → Garden → *Copy the link to your garden*). Open the link on another device (an iPad, say) and the same garden is there; from then on, whichever device last changed it is the one the others take on when they open. Anyone with the link can open and change your garden, so keep it to yourself. Safari wipes a site's stored data after seven days without a visit; the online copy outlives that.
- **A room's garden** is kept online by its code. Whoever has been in the room longest saves it every 15 seconds while it changes. Someone who opens the room when nobody is in it gets the room's garden as it was left (as a guest: their own is set aside and comes back when they leave), so a room is a place you can go back to and keep building. A room never saved starts from the garden of whoever opens it, as before. *Start a new room* asks the worker for a code no saved garden has. If a room's saved garden can't be fetched, you're put in your own and the room's is never saved over.
- **Earlier saves** (Settings → Garden): the worker keeps each garden's last twelve saves; pick one to go back to it (in a room, only when nobody else is there).
- **Garden files**: *Save to a file* downloads the whole garden (gzip'd JSON); *Load a garden file* puts one in place of yours. No server involved.
- What travels: the garden in four parts (the wall and free builds; the planting, trees included; the junk heap; the junk's pieces, the heavy part, which change least). A save sends only the parts that changed. The worker stores each part once, by its SHA-256, in a SQLite-backed Durable Object per garden, and refuses saves closer together than 4 s or bigger than 24 MB. The worker is live at `garden-worlds.samuelling.workers.dev`, deployed by Cloudflare Workers Builds from this repo's `worker/` folder: a push to `main` that changes `worker/` redeploys it (nothing else does). `?noworlds` keeps to the browser only; `?worlds=http://127.0.0.1:8787` points at a local worker (`npx wrangler dev` in `worker/`).

## Together (up to six)

**Enter together** on the title (or the **together** button, Shift+G) opens the lobby: your name, your suit, and a room. **Start a new room** gives a four-letter code and a link (`linglab.org/blackhole/?room=ABCD`); a friend who opens the link gets **join room ABCD** on the title and lands in the same garden. Up to six astronauts at once.

- **Suits** (`src/outfits.js`): ten outfits (Apollo, Pumpkin, Mercury, Mars, Midnight, Sakura, Mint, Lemon, Cobalt, Moss), and then each of the suit's five colours is yours to pick: the shell, the trim (joints, belt, boots), the patches, the visor and the glow. The suit is painted in those five flat colours and an outfit repaints each in the cel shader, so it costs nothing to draw. **On the helmet** you can wear an antenna, a fin, cat or bunny ears, a halo, horns, a propeller (it spins), a crown or a flower: small shapes built on the helmet's crown in the suit's own colours (`body.js`, `topperParts`). While the lobby is open the camera turns round so you can see yourself in it. Your look is remembered and you wear it alone as well. Other suit shapes are a job for Blender: see *Other suits* below.
- **The others** are full copies of your own body (`body.js`), walked by the same gait, hands and fingers from where they are and what they're doing: each browser sends its position, heading, gaze and wall gestures about 15 times a second (a few dozen bytes, unreliable and unordered so a lost packet is simply skipped), and each end plays the others back a tenth of a second late, interpolated. Name tags float over their helmets.
- **Voices**, as in MEGAjam: WebRTC audio (Opus, echo cancelling, noise suppression, level control), sent only while you talk: hold **`** or the **🎙 hold to talk** button, or tick open mic. Each voice comes from that astronaut's helmet in 3-D (HRTF): nearer is louder and you can hear where they are; "hear everyone as if they were close" turns that off. Someone talking lights their name tag and pulses their suit's glow strips. Voices don't go through the garden's sound switch. Headphones help. Each person can be muted in the lobby.
- **The sky** follows whoever has been in the room longest: hour and day of the year, every few seconds ("share the sky" turns it off for you).
- **One garden.** Everyone in a room builds, plants and digs in the same garden: that of whoever was in the room first (so the person whose garden it is to be should start the room). Someone coming in is handed it as it stands (the wall, free builds and their glue, the stone heaps, the junk heap, the planting); their own garden is set aside untouched and comes back when they leave. Only the browser the garden belongs to saves it, including what the others built, and including what was built while its owner was away if they come back to the same room. Clearing things wholesale (the wall, the free builds, the junk heap, the planting) is the owner's alone.
- **Building together** (`src/walling.js`, "building together"):
  - A stint or free build that somebody else is working appears as they have it, live: every stone a mesh that follows what they send, the stone in their beam moving with it (15 times a second, riding their body's stream).
  - **B at the same place joins them**: both of you then simulate the site. Each sends the stones it adds, the stone it holds, and where stones come to rest after what it did (four times a second, only what moved more than about a centimetre); the other sets its own copies there. Stone lying still is the same for everyone, to a few millimetres; a stone in flight is each simulation's own until it lands. If two of you grab the same stone, one keeps it.
  - Glue is told (seams and all), so is every stone taken from a heap. Putting a site away while others are at it leaves it with them; finishing a stint fixes it for everybody. A builder who drops out leaves their site as it stood.
  - A stone has a name within its site that says where it came from (the site's pile by its seed, a heap by its place, the junk by its piece), and everyone grows the same stone from the same name, so only names and positions travel: a fresh stint's pile of about 375 stones is some 45 KB, once; a stone set down is a few dozen bytes.
- **The junk heap** is the room's: whoever has been there longest fetches from the Shred Hole's mailbox for everyone and says what came through and where it landed; taking a piece, putting it back and vaporizing are told too.
- **Planting** and digging (yours and the garden's own) are told as they happen.
- **Not shared:** the voids each of you opens.
- **Through the relay** (a pair that can't link directly): the garden is handed over without the junk already on the heap (its shapes are the heavy part, and the relay is Cloudflare's), so that pair sees later deliveries but not earlier ones, and a build made of earlier junk shows with those pieces missing.
- **Checked** by `tools/test/share.mjs` and `junkshare.mjs`: two real instances of the walling and junk code joined by a bus, in node (a stint mirrored and co-built, glue, records, a newcomer, someone vanishing, a guest leaving; deliveries, lifting, putting back, vaporizing). In two browsers through the local relay: the hand-over, a stint mirrored stone for stone, the held stone, joining a stint, a guest's stone reaching the host, and the guest's own garden coming back. Not checked: two real machines over a direct link.
- **Network** (`src/room.js`, from MEGAjam): the Cloudflare relay `crush-commando-mp` (a Durable Object that sleeps when idle) carries only the handshake, in rooms named `gd-<code>`; after that every pair links directly by WebRTC, so walking and voices don't pass through Cloudflare. A pair whose networks won't connect directly (some strict or carrier NATs) falls back to the relay for movement at four updates a second, and can't hear each other (there's no TURN server). `?server=ws://localhost:8787` points at MEGAjam's local relay (`node server/dev.js` in that repo) for testing.
- The microphone may be blocked when the garden is viewed inside Claude; at linglab.org it asks normally.

### Other suits

The room panel (Shift+G) has a row of suits to wear, alone or together; each is fetched the first time someone in the garden wears it (0.5 to 1.4 MB), and everyone else sees it. They are made from NASA's free models (github.com/nasa/NASA-3D-Resources, `3D Models/`: free of copyright; NASA's usage guidelines apply, so nothing here implies NASA's endorsement, and the NASA insignia and wordmark are not carried over):

- **ACES**: the Shuttle's orange launch-and-entry suit. **Mark III**: the hard-shell prototype of the 1980s. **Mercury** and **Gemini**: the early pressure suits. **EVA suit**: a plain spacewalk suit with its life-support pack. **Z-2**: the planetary prototype with the bubble helmet.
- Not converted: the *Extravehicular Mobility Unit* (the model is drawn seated in its jetpack, which shares its materials with the suit, so the two have to be parted by hand), and the Smithsonian's scan of Armstrong's Apollo 11 suit (a scan: very heavy, one piece).
- `tools/blender/build_suits.py` makes them (bpy; `tools/undraco.mjs` first undoes the models' Draco compression). The body (`body.js`) is not a skinned mesh but fifteen rigid parts on a rig, so each model is stood up facing forward at eye height, cut into those parts (limb by limb, with joints set by eye per suit), each limb turned about its joint until it hangs straight down, the joints closed with a ball, the surface repainted in the suit's five flat colours (so any outfit repaints any suit; a textured model's faces are sorted into those colours by what the texture shows), thinned to about thirty thousand triangles, and given the first suit's gloves, whose fingers the app knows how to move. `blender/suit_<id>.blend` holds each result for working on by hand.
- What that costs: a limb is rigid, so a sleeve does not crease at the elbow, it turns on a ball; where a model was drawn with its arms out, the shoulders keep some of that width; the gloves and their fingers are the garden suit's on every suit.

### How it moves: motion capture

The body walks, runs, stands about and gestures from recordings of people (`src/motion.js`, `assets/motion.json`, made by `tools/mocap/build_motion.py`).

- **Fifteen bearings** to choose from in the room panel ("how you carry yourself"): easy, proud, happy, brisk, broad, light, skipping, musical, swagger, giant, shy, sad, tired, old, angry. Each is one recorded stride of walking and one of running in that manner. *By the rules* is the body's earlier walk, worked out from rules alone; it is also what you get if the motion file cannot be fetched.
- **Standing**, the body no longer holds a pose: it stands the way a person waiting does, one stretch of standing easing into the next, and after a long while it thinks, stretches or nods.
- **Gestures** on the number keys: 1 wave, 2 bow, 3 beckon, 4 this way, 5 nod, 6 arms up, 7 laugh, 8 dance, 9 chicken dance, 0 stretch (more in the room panel). Standing, a gesture is the whole body; walking, it is from the waist up. Others in the room see them, and see how you carry yourself.
- **Pace.** You walk (1.6 m/s) and Shift runs (5.6 m/s). A controller's stick, or a thumb dragged on a phone, goes at whatever pace it is pushed to: a lean strolls, half way is the walk, all the way is the run, and the body moves through its recorded walk into its recorded run as it does.
- How a recording becomes the body's: nothing is taken from a file's rest pose or joint angles (the libraries disagree about both). Each frame is run forward to world space, and a frame of reference is fixed to every bone that drives a part of the rig, calibrated once on a standing frame. Kept per frame, in the wearer's own frame: the pelvis's place and turn, the chest, head, arms and hands, and for each leg where the ankle is, how the foot is turned and which way the knee points. Legs are solved to the ankle each frame, so one stride serves legs of any length and can be lengthened or shortened to the speed you are going. Slower than the recording, steps shorten and slow together; between its walk and its run the two are mixed; faster than its run, the stride lengthens and then quickens.
- **Sources.** The gaits and most gestures: Bandai Namco Research Inc., *Bandai-Namco-Research-Motiondataset* (github.com/BandaiNamcoResearchInc/Bandai-Namco-Research-Motiondataset), **CC BY-NC 4.0: credit them, and no commercial use**. Standing, stretching, laughing, the chicken dance: the CMU Graphics Lab Motion Capture Database (mocap.cs.cmu.edu), free for any use, in B. Hahne's BVH conversion. If the garden is ever sold or put behind a paywall, the Bandai Namco motions have to come out (`build_motion.py` lists which are which).
- **From inside.** Your own head is never drawn for you, so looking down there was a hole where it joins: the collar now has a floor in it (a neck ring in the suit's trim colour). The body is hung from its neck, so a lean or a run no longer slides the collar out from under your gaze, and standing, the chest is kept square under where you look (a recorded way of standing has it turned off to one side).
- Not done: walking backwards and sideways use the forward stride turned round; there is no stepping when you turn on the spot; at speeds far from the recording's the feet slide a little; a gesture does not move the camera (bow and you still look straight ahead); the fingers are still the hands' own.

### Plants modelled in Blender

Every species in the garden, all 68 of them, is modelled in Blender, built by script from reference photographs (`tools/blender/build_plants.py`). Each species gets `assets/plants/<key>.json` (the model), an atlas and a normal map, plus `blender/plants/<key>.blend` for hand work.

- **From photographs.** Each species' habit, counts (rays, petals, whorls, leaflets), proportions and colours were read from openly licensed iNaturalist photographs (CC0 and CC BY only, credited in `assets/plants/CREDITS.md`) and sized to the heights and spreads in `src/looks.js` and `src/flora.js`. Nothing is a scan or a cut-out photo: leaves, petals and plumes are outlines drawn on curved strips and plates, with veins, teeth and colour painted by the script.
- **Six by hand, the rest by description.** Coneflower, cranesbill, lavender, catmint, coreopsis and moor grass are written out in `build_plants.py`. The other 62 share forms: `tools/blender/plantforms.py` has kinds of leaf (simple, strap, plate, dissected, compound, ternate, digitate), florets, heads (daisies, spikes, balls, umbels, plumes, cups, trumpets) and habits (a clump of leafy stems, a dome of stalked leaves, a fountain of blades). `tools/blender/species2.py` describes each species in those terms; a few flowers (columbine, iris, rose, cattail, goldenrod) are built individually.
- **Three variants a species**, each a plant's foliage, its stems, and where its flower heads go; the heads are meshes of their own. Every part also has a light version.
- **Distance decides** (`src/plantmodels.js`): within 8 m and roughly ahead of you a plant is the full model; out to 22 m in your view it's the light one; beyond that or behind you it's the garden's own procedural plant. A plant drawn as a model is flagged in the garden's plant table, and its procedural parts fold away in the shader, so you never see both. At the busiest spots the models add about a million triangles.
- **Fetched as you go.** The garden opens without waiting for them. `assets/plants/index.json` lists what exists, and species are fetched two at a time, nearest first, once a plant of theirs is within 35 m (25 on a phone). All species share one set of shader programs. A species nobody has been near for a while gives its textures back to the GPU.
- **Seasons and wind** work on them as on the rest of the garden: foliage comes up and dies back, stems stand while there are flowers or seed, heads open with the bloom and brown into seed heads, and everything sways.
- **Size.** About 15 MB of models for all 68 (roughly half that over the wire), but you only fetch the ones near where you walk. The `.blend` files for the 62 described species are not kept in the repository: `build_plants.py` makes them again.
- `?nopm` turns them off, for comparison.

### The fountains

The central fountain and the two on the cross walk are turned and carved in Blender (`tools/blender/build_stone.py`, written to `assets/models/stone.json`). The centrepiece is three scalloped, gadrooned basins diminishing up a column of acanthus-carved balusters to a pine-cone finial 4.6 m up. It stands on an octagonal plinth with four scroll consoles, and four shells send low jets arcing into the pool. The bowl fountains have an acanthus baluster, an egg-and-dart lip and a small pine cone. Each piece is weathered slightly and its creases shaded by an ambient-occlusion bake. The garden fits its water, falling sheets and jets to the basins' measurements.

## The pond and the brook

A spring-fed pond lies west of the garden, just beyond the wall line, about twelve metres across and waist deep at the middle. A brook leaves its east end, goes under the wall line beneath a stone lintel (so a stint of wall can stand over it), and wanders across the garden's north lawns: through the field of light stalks, under a slab footbridge on each walk it meets, among the globes, and out under the east wall. From there it meanders north-east through the wood, drops down a run of white water where the land falls away, and goes over the edge at the land's lowest corner: about thirty metres of fall, tearing into strands and then mist before it reaches the bubble's skin. 162 m from pond to lip.

- One description drives all of it (`src/brook.js`): a centre line through hand-set points, a width at each, and a water level that only goes down (never flatter than 1 in 600; it follows the ground where the ground falls faster). The channel is cut into the terrain from those, so you can wade it; banks are laid back further where they are higher.
- The water is drawn carried along by the current: the ripple pattern rides downstream at the speed of the water at that point (faster mid-stream and where it is steep), with white water at the edges and on the steep run. The bed shows through, dimmer and greener with depth. The fall's pattern is laid out in time of flight, so it accelerates as falling water does.
- It is made to look found and not built: the line shifts a few hand-widths side to side between its set points, the width swells and narrows, pools lie deeper where it is wide, the grass grows down the banks to the water, and there are stones along the edge and in the steep run, lily pads on the still side of the pond, and its own planting on the banks (sedges, iris, marsh marigold, ferns, hostas, bulrush and a giant rhubarb at the pond; thin inside the garden, thick outside).
- The water is clear. You see the real bed, the stones, and the **koi**: sixteen of them (nine on a phone), seven in the pond and the rest in the brook above the steep run, in five colourings (red on white, the same with black flecks, plain gold, black-based, orange and white). In the pond they swim for a point, idle, and choose another. In the brook they travel to a place, hold there nose to the current, and move on. Come within a couple of metres and they bolt. The swimming is a wave run down the body in the shader, faster and wider with speed.
- The garden's plants, the light stalks, the globes and the wood's trees keep out of the water. You cannot plant in it.
- You hear it: the brook as you come near, the fall from further off.

Not modelled: the water is not a simulation (it does not rise in rain, part round a stone, or freeze), there are no reflections of the garden in it (the formal pools have those), what you see through it is not bent by the surface, the koi do not avoid each other or the stones, and nothing floats down it yet.

## Planting

**P** (or the *plant* button) opens the plant menu: about 130 perennials in ten kinds of garden (cottage border, prairie and meadow, grasses, woodland shade, Mediterranean, desert, tropical, water's edge, alpine and rock, bulbs). Pick one and a ring follows your aim over the ground: the outer ring is the plant's full spread, the inner one the pot you are setting out. Click plants it, **X** digs up the nearest of yours, **Q** puts the trowel away. Up to 800, kept in your browser.

What is modelled, per species:

- **Size:** mature height and spread as nurseries give them, from a 15 cm alpine cushion to a 3 m banana.
- **Growth:** a plant goes in at pot size and takes its own number of years to fill out (two for catmint, five for a peony, eight for an agave), slow at first, then fast, then levelling off. It runs on the garden's calendar, so the season speed and the voids' slowed time apply to it.
- **Season:** the months it flowers, and how it winters: dying back, standing as straw, keeping its leaves, or dropping them.
- **Spacing:** each plant claims a circle of its full spread. Planted closer than that to a neighbour (yours or the garden's own) it still takes, but grows smaller, down to half size; the ring turns amber to say so. Closer than about four tenths of the proper distance, or on paving, gravel, water or stone, it is refused (red).

**Digging up and replanting.** With a plant in hand (the trowel out), look at any plant in a bed, yours or the garden's own, and **X** digs it up; its ground is freed at once, so something else can go in where it stood. The garden's own plants you dig up stay dug up (remembered in your browser, with your planting); Settings → Garden → *Put back the garden's own plants you dug up* restores them. Trees aren't dug.

Not modelled: hardiness, soil, sun and shade, water. Inside the bubble a canna is not killed by a Massachusetts winter; it dies down and comes back like any other perennial. The new species are drawn from the same parts as the garden's own (six plant forms, the scanned leaf shapes, the painted flower styles) at their own sizes and colours, so an agave is recognisably a rosette of wide swords and not a botanical model of one.

### Trees, and giants

The plant menu has two more kinds: **Trees** (sugar maple, red oak, red maple, paper birch, linden, sweetgum, ginkgo, weeping willow) and **Giant trees** (the same eight). Each goes in as a whip about 1.9 m tall and grows on its own clock like everything else here (the season speed runs it; a void nearby slows it), along a curve that rises quickly and levels off: most of its height in 10 to 45 years, depending on its kind. A tree grows to the height its kind reaches in a garden (14 m for the willow, 24 for the oak and the sugar maple). It is drawn from the wood's own Blender forms (`src/arbor.js`), narrow in the crown while young; its leaves turn and fall with its species.

**Giants** keep growing, slowly, for a century or more. A tree's height on Earth is capped by how high it can lift water: sap is pulled up the wood by evaporation from the leaves, and the column must hold its own weight against gravity, so the tallest trees' top leaves are always water-starved (Koch et al. 2004, *Nature*, put the limit at 122 to 130 m for coast redwoods). In the bubble, at 4.2 m/s², the same column can stand about 2.3 times as tall, so a giant heads for 2.3 times the height its kind reaches at its tallest in the wild (60 to 95 m), and stops 7 m short of the bubble's skin wherever that comes first. A big crown is filled by more boughs rather than bigger ones (each bough at most about twice its size). Trees want room: 3 m from any trunk, 6 for a giant, and not on paths or by the water; you can plant 40. **X** with the trowel out digs one up. They are saved and shared like the rest of your planting.

## The great void

The voids are restless. Each has a lifetime drawn at random (fifteen minutes on average, twenty-five for the great one, so that somewhere in the garden one goes about every two minutes; with no memory: an old void is no likelier to go than a young one), and when it is up the void evaporates by itself, wherever you are. It then lies dormant for one and a half to four minutes and gathers again within a few metres of where it first hung, up to a quarter larger or smaller. One you evaporate yourself comes back sooner, in half a minute to two. Settings → Voids → *Restless voids* turns this off, and the voids go back to their fixed places and times.

A giant black hole hangs 12 m above the grand fountain. It never fully sleeps; walk within ~40 m and it wakes, draining the central garden while an accretion disk of stolen light orbits it (inner edge white-hot, outer amber, the approaching side brighter). The lensing pass bends the far side of the disk over the top of the shadow and lets the near side cross in front; the disk's glow lights the fountain and parterre below, and the whole thing hangs reflected in the pool.

## Time

Every plant, tree and patch of ground cover has its own clock. A static clock at distance r from a void ticks at √(1 − Σ rₛ/r) of the garden's calendar rate (Schwarzschild time dilation, weak-field potentials summed over all voids; rₛ = 2.6 × the void's horizon radius, so the effect grows as a void wakes and feeds). Each frame, each plant integrates its own proper time and its phenology is read from that, not from the calendar: near an awake void the border lags behind the season, and under the giant the parterre can still be in bloom while the outer garden has gone bare.

You have a clock too. Your watch is real time; the garden's calendar advances at 1/√(1 − Σ rₛ/r) of that while you stand in a well, so the rest of the garden runs fast for you, its light and the sky arrive blueshifted, the music rises in pitch, and the HUD tallies how much younger than the garden you've become. Light from deeper wells than yours arrives redshifted.

**Seasons:** S cycles paused / 1 day / 1 week / 1 month per second; the slider scrubs the calendar (which also sets the sun's height); R replants so you can watch three years of establishment. Phenology comes from Shortwood Meadow's sourced bloom months: spring emergence (early and late species), bloom windows, summer-dormant alliums and poppies, standing seed heads, grasses that stand as straw until cut back in February, evergreen lavender, pinks and lamb's ears, woody roses and hydrangea, deciduous trees with spring flush, autumn colour and leaf fall, and a lawn that greens, dries and dulls through the year.

## The garden

Small on purpose, so every surface can be close-up quality. Materials are photo-scans; stonework is modelled in Blender; plants are procedural structure dressed in scanned leaves.

- **Layout:** an iron gate in a brick wall; a gravel forecourt; a tunnel of light arches through double herbaceous borders backed by yew; a canal garden with arching jets, yew columns, box hedges and lavender walks; a round central garden with a three-tier fountain, urns and box-edged parterre beds inside a yew ring; a loop walk with deep borders against the ivy-patched wall; two bowl fountains, rose arches, a bench niche, two long-grass meadows, six trees, ten cast-iron lamp posts.
- **Planting design:** a clipped English frame (box balls on a seven-metre beat, yew cones and rose obelisks behind, topiary lawns, standards, box-edged parterre) holding loose, layered matrix planting inside it, after Oudolf & Kingsbury, Rainer & West and Dunnett. Every bed is built in four layers: **structure** (about 10%: long-lived plants with strong form in odd-numbered groups on a steady beat, every third group stepping forward), **theme** (about 25%: the showy seasonal plants in long, thin, diagonal drifts that recur along the bed), **scatter** (about 5–10%: see-through emergents such as Verbena bonariensis, drumstick alliums and Knautia, dotted singly, front included, so no bed is a height staircase) and **matrix** (the rest: a low, knitting ground layer, mostly a grass, planted tight so no soil shows). Palettes follow aspect: a Beth Chatto-style dry garden of silvers and blues against the sun-baked north wall; a hot late-summer prairie (Sporobolus matrix) facing the evening sun on the east; a cool Oudolf mix of blues, plums and pinks on the west; a green-and-white woodland edge (Hakonechloa, Astrantia, Japanese anemones, hydrangeas) in the shadow of the south wall. The entrance double border is mirrored across the walk, with Calamagrostis standing in pairs like a colonnade. The parterre is lavender-edged, with roses on a regular beat through a matrix of catmint and lady's mantle and alliums rising through. The two long-grass areas are prairie meadows of Sporobolus and Schizachyrium with coneflowers, rudbeckias, liatris and Knautia. Grasses and seed heads stand through winter. Ten plants were added for this (Sesleria, Deschampsia, Hakonechloa, Molinia, Verbena bonariensis, Sanguisorba, Persicaria, Knautia, Astrantia, Allium sphaerocephalon).
- **Ground:** four scanned PBR materials (lawn, mulch, gravel, granite setts: colour, normal and AO/roughness maps) blended by two painted masks, with noise-broken edges, setts edging along every path, painted contact shadows under everything, and wet stone round the fountains. Gravel is pale and compacted down the trodden middle of each path and darker, mossier and littered toward its edges.
- **Lawn:** ~360k GPU grass blades in two tiers that follow the walker — curved nine-vertex blades with rounded normals, clumping, mowing stripes, and long golden grass in the meadows.
- **Stonework (Blender):** fountains, pool kerbs of jointed coping stones, urns and pier finials are built by `tools/blender/build_stone.py` — turned profiles carved with gadroons, flutes and pinecone scales, weathered, simplified, with ambient occlusion baked into vertex colours. `blender/stone.blend` opens in Blender. In the garden they wear a scanned limestone, projected triplanar, with moss on upward faces, rain streaks and a wet variant.
- **Plants:** 57 perennials, bulbs, grasses, shrubs and roses from [Shortwood Meadow](https://github.com/samuelling/shortwood-meadow) (`src/plantart.js`, `src/looks.js`) — plant structure and painted flowers — now with scanned leaves (colour + normal maps) for every leaf type. ~3,000 plants draw as instanced meshes with distance LOD and wind sway.
- **Showpiece flowers in true 3D** (`src/flowers3d.js`): English-rose rosettes, double peonies, crinkled poppies, allium globes, delphinium, lupine and foxglove spires built floret by floret.
- **Hedges & topiary:** swept, clipped forms shelled with sprigs, some growing out of the face and some lying against it. The yew sprig is baked in Blender (`tools/blender/bake_yew.py`): a fan of sprays modelled needle by needle, rendered from above with Cycles for colour and for a normal map. Box uses scanned twigs.
- **Trees (Blender):** `tools/blender/build_trees.py` grows three variants as a skeleton of trunk, scaffold limbs, branches and twigs reaching for the crown's surface. Trunk, root buttresses and the base of each limb are fused by a voxel remesh, so limbs flow out of the trunk and roots flare into the ground. About 1,700 boughs of scanned lime leaves sit on the twigs of each tree, and the sky each bough (and each patch of bark) can see is ray-cast through the crown and stored as vertex colour, so canopies are bright outside and dim within. The garden instances them, scaled to each tree's crown, with bark projected triplanar and leaf normals leaned outward so a crown lights as one rounded mass. `blender/trees.blend` opens in Blender.
- **Props:** scanned lamp posts, gate, bench and a stone cat on the pool rim; coping stones (laid end to end along the wall and canal, each its own tone) and path lanterns modelled in Blender (`tools/blender/build_props.py`).
- **You (Blender):** `tools/blender/build_astronaut.py` models the suit the way a hard suit is built: rigid shells joined by bearing rings, a dark ball at every joint, chest unit, wrist display, life-support pack, helmet. Fifteen segments, each with its origin at its joint, so `src/body.js` poses it by turning whole segments: legs by two-bone IK to feet that stay planted through each stride, arms swinging against them, cadence and stride from how fast you actually moved. Shading is cel: light arrives in flat bands (sun or moon with a hard-edged shadow, the exhibit's glow, sky, a small suit lamp), compressed so the suit never blows out, and each shell is drawn again inside-out and swollen along smoothed normals for an ink line of constant width on screen. The voids drain it like everything else; the instruments keep glowing. You never see your own helmet, but your shadow has it.
- **Water:** one planar reflection pass shared by every pool and the canal (the voids appear in it as black spheres), a refracted stone floor with caustics, ripples, ribbons of falling water, foam and droplet jets.
- **Light:** sun or moon with soft shadows following you, a sky with drifting cloud, environment lighting, fog; the exhibit's light pools and the voids' drain are injected into every material.
- **Rendering:** 4× multisampling with alpha-to-coverage (smooth leaf edges without sorting), screen-space ambient occlusion from the depth buffer, bloom, a light colour grade, ACES tone mapping. Quality is set per machine in the Graphics tab (below); `?gfx=`, `?q=low`, `?msaa=0` and `?ao=0` override it for one visit.

- **Light:** the place under the sky (latitude and longitude, *use my location*, or back to Watertown, MA), the faintest stars shown, moon size, sky mode (night, live, set) and hour, exhibit lights, moonlight, exposure, glow, haze and cloud.
- **Graphics:** presets and every quality setting (see below).
- **Hands:** Invisible Hands. Start or join a room and another app, or the console on a phone (https://linglab.org/invisiblehands/console/), can move 20 of the controls (season speed, day of the year, garden age, wind, gusts, pull, exact clocks, void size, light eaten, lensing, disk, streams, open a void, evaporate the nearest, hour, exhibit lights, moonlight, exposure, glow, cloud), each marked with the Invisible Hands tag. ## Autumn

The trees turn and drop their leaves by the pigment model from [Turning Grove](https://claude.ai/artifact/4EFFv4EkuWYdEv33kaUP1Z) (`src/senescence.js`), leaf by leaf.

- **Onset** follows Delpierre et al. (2009): once daylength falls below a species' threshold, cold-degree-days accumulate until a critical sum is reached; drought adds to it, and the day length comes from the latitude set in the Light tab.
- **Colour** is four pigments per leaf: chlorophyll is dismantled (faster in cold bright weather), carotenoids are unmasked, anthocyanin is built from sugar trapped by bright days and cold nights, tannins brown the dying leaf. The colour drawn is the light those pigments let back out (Beer-Lambert), so a half-turned maple leaf is orange because red sits over a yellowing blade.
- **Every leaf is its own**: each bough's sun exposure is the sky visibility baked in Blender, so the outside of a crown turns first and reddest; each leaf on a twig card has its own identity (`trees_atlas_m`), turning and falling on its own day; within a leaf the tissue along the veins runs about a week behind the margins.
- **Fall**: an abscission layer forms as the green goes; rain and frost finish it. Leaves come down through the air shaped and coloured like the tree they left, drift with the wind, are pulled toward an awake void, and gather as litter under the crowns, on the paths and afloat on the pools and canal. The litter browns, and is gone by spring. Red oak holds a fifth of its leaves, brown, through the winter; ginkgo lets go all at once after the first hard frost.
- **Species**: small-leaved lime and weeping willow (the garden's original trees, with new parameters), plus red maple, sweetgum, paper birch and ginkgo planted in the garden, and sugar maple and red oak with them in the belt beyond the wall. In an ordinary year at 42°N colour peaks from about 10 October (red maple) to mid-November (ginkgo, willow).
- **Weather**: each garden year gets its own generated weather. Garden tab → Autumn has autumn temperature, sunshine, rainfall and an early hard frost, with this year's dates per species. With live weather on, the last three months of real days at your place (Open-Meteo) are laid over the generated year for the first garden year. A tree near a void runs on its own slower clock, so it turns late.
- Parameters are illustrative (tuned to typical timing), not fitted to data. Chlorophyll's green absorbance is higher than Turning Grove's, so summer leaves reflect about 15% in the green as real ones do.

Leaf cards are drawn by `tools/build_leafcards.py` from botanical outlines (Turning Grove's six shapes plus lime and willow); `tools/blender/build_trees.py` now grows six forms (two limes, willow, birch, oak, and a spire for ginkgo and sweetgum).

**Leaf size goes with the tree.** Each tree is one of six Blender forms scaled to its own crown, and its leaves take one scale from that: the cube root of the tree's volume against the form it was grown from, the same in every direction (so a tall, narrow tree's leaves are not stretched). A garden tree is close to 1, where a lime leaf is about 8 cm and a red oak's 16 cm; a tree in the wood one and a half times the size carries leaves one and a half times the size. Leaves in the air and on the ground use the same number, so what lies under a tree matches what hangs on it. Boughs are never enlarged to fill out a crown: where a tree needs more cover it carries more leaves, and where a graphics preset thins the boughs the crown is simply thinner.

## The grass

Real blades are drawn only round the walker, on turf whose texture takes over beyond them. They come in three rings (12, 28 and 80 m across) drawn as one sward: past about two metres the blades thin out smoothly (as 1/d²) while each widens a little (as √d), every blade kept or dropped by its own dice against the density wanted where it stands, and a blade near its threshold is short, so they grow in as you come and never pop. (The earlier two rings fell from about 1,240 blades a square metre to 70 at five metres from you: a circle of lush grass that followed you about.) Far blades turn to face you.

- **Inside the walls** the lawn is medium mown, with mowing stripes. Mown grass is too short to make out past about 15 m, so there it shrinks away into the turf between 9 and 17 m.
- **Out in the field** the grass is mown too, with **swaths of meadow**: long sweeping bands of tall grass (up to about 0.9 m, moving in the wind, some blades carrying straw-coloured seed heads) with mown rides between them, over a little under half the field. A few metres' margin outside the wall line and the wormhole's clearing stay mown. Tall grass keeps every blade each ring has and carries on to 27 m, thinning away by 39 m. The swaths are `swathF` in `src/field.js`.

## Weather

By default the garden has the weather that the place under the sky is having now (Light tab; Watertown, MA unless you change it): cloud cover, wind speed, direction and gustiness, rain, snow, fog, and snow already lying. Conditions come from [Open-Meteo](https://open-meteo.com) (free, no key), asked again every ten minutes. Only the latitude and longitude are sent, rounded to two decimals (about a kilometre).

- **Rain and snow** fall in a box that travels with you. Each streak and flake is lit by the light where it is, so rain glints in the exhibit's colours, goes dark inside a void's reach and bends toward the horizon on its way down. The wind slants it.
- **Overcast** is a solid grey deck that hides the sun, moon and stars and cuts the direct light; fog, rain and snow thicken the haze.
- **Snow settles** on whatever faces the sky (lawn, paths, hedge tops, stone, leaves), patchily at first, and melts when it stops. **Rain** darkens and glosses the ground, which dries slowly.
- **Sound:** rain hiss and a low wind that rises with the gusts.
- **By hand:** Garden tab → Weather. Moving Rain, Snow, Snow lying, Wind, Gusts or Cloud takes over from the live weather, starting from what it is at that moment; *local, live* hands it back.
- **Where it works:** a page hosted on claude.ai may not call outside services, so there the live weather is unavailable and the sliders are used; the GitHub Pages copy (or any ordinary web host) can reach it. No thunder or lightning yet.

**Make it now** (Garden tab) sets today's date, a real-time calendar, the live sky and the live weather in one press. *Real time* on its own runs the seasons at one day per day; the season button on the main controls steps through it too.

## The sky

The sky is the real one over the chosen place (Watertown, MA by default; north is toward the bench, south toward the gate).

- **Sun** by a standard solar-position formula, with refraction near the horizon.
- **Moon** by a truncated Meeus lunar series with parallax: position to about 0.2°, at its true size (half a degree; the *moon size* setting enlarges it), lit by the real sun direction so its phase, terminator and tilt are right, with faint earthshine. Moonlight on the garden follows its phase and height: new-moon nights are darker.
- **Stars:** 5,044 Hipparcos stars to magnitude 6 with their true colours (from B–V), precessed to the date, dimmed and set twinkling more near the horizon. The *faintest stars* setting stands in for light pollution: around magnitude 3.5–4 under Watertown's real sky.
- **Planets:** Mercury, Venus, Mars, Jupiter and Saturn from JPL's approximate orbital elements, with their brightness.
- **Milky Way** along the true galactic plane, brightest toward Sagittarius.

Checked against PyEphem: stars to 0.01°, planets to about 0.05°, the moon to about 0.2° and its phase to 0.1%. In *live* mode it is the sky right now. In *night* and *set* modes it is the sky for the garden's calendar date at that hour, in your browser's time zone.

## How it works

- **Drain field:** every light, surface and ground fragment computes `L = Π (1 − sᵢ·fᵢ(d))` over up to 12 voids, where `s` is each void's (slowly decaying) strength and `f` a smooth falloff whose reach grows as it wakes.
- **Infall:** light points inside a void's reach are rotated around its axis and pulled toward it in the vertex shader; a separate GPU particle system spirals light from the surrounding ground into the horizon, tinted by the local light colours and blue-shifting as it falls.
- **Lensing (exact Schwarzschild optics):** each void's black disc is its shadow, so its critical impact parameter is the disc radius and its horizon is `rs = R / 2.598`. For every pixel the post pass finds the ray's impact parameter `b` and bends it by the true Schwarzschild angle `α(b)`, tabulated at load by integrating the null geodesic (it reduces to `2rs/b` far out and diverges logarithmically at the photon sphere, which is what wraps the garden's lights into arcs and a photon ring). Sources close behind a void are displaced less (thin-lens factor `D_ls/D_s`); things in front are untouched. Rays bent out of the frame sample a cube map rendered round the walker, one face every other frame while a void is awake (`?env=0` turns it off).
- **The giant's disk:** ray-traced in the same pass. In the plane of each ray, `u = 1/r` obeys `u'' + u = (3/2) rs u²`; the shader steps it round the hole and catches each crossing of the disk plane, so the far side of the disk arches over and under the shadow. The disk runs from the innermost stable orbit (3 rs) outward with a thin-disk brightness profile, orbital Doppler beaming and gravitational redshift.
- **Clocks:** a static clock at distance `r` ticks at `sqrt(1 − rs/r)`. The **clocks** button switches between *exact* (the horizon the optics imply: time slows a few percent at most) and *×7* (each horizon enlarged about sevenfold so the slowing can be felt; the default).
- **Evaporation (click a void):** clicking a void (or tapping it on a phone) makes it evaporate by Hawking radiation, sped up enormously. A black hole radiates like a body at a temperature inversely proportional to its mass, and loses mass at a rate proportional to 1/M², so M³ falls linearly: M(t) = M₀(1 − t/τ)^(1/3). It shrinks slowly, then all at once, glowing hotter and brighter (red, then white, then blue-white) as it goes, while the light it took seeps back, and it ends in a flash. A real hole this size would take about 10^57 years, and since it is colder than the cosmic microwave background it would in fact be growing. Voids you opened are gone for good; the garden's own re-form after 45 seconds (the great void after 75).
- **What is not physics:** the drain of light and the pull on the walker are the piece's fiction. A real hole this size would weigh about 150 Earths.
- **Ground light:** pools of light are baked into a canvas light map at load and multiplied by the drain field per fragment.
- **Sound (Web Audio):** a chord pad whose filter and level follow the light where you stand, glints that fade as the light does, a sub-rumble near awake voids, and an inhale when one wakes. Under it, the meadow from Dry Stack (`src/meadow.js`): wind in three bands that gusts by itself and rises with the weather, New England birds by day (chickadee, white-throated sparrow, blue jay, crow, song sparrow; fewer in winter, none in rain), crickets on nights from June to October, an owl now and then. Settings → Garden → Sound: *Chimes and tones* off leaves only the natural sound; *Meadow sounds* off leaves only the exhibit's.

ES modules, no build step; three.js r160 from jsDelivr; about 19 MB of textures and models in `assets/`.
